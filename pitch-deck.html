Guide Pitch deck

Build a deck they remember.

A pitch deck is a short set of slides that explains what your startup does, why it matters and what you need, so judges, mentors and investors can decide whether to support you. A good deck tells one clear story, and each slide answers the next question your audience would ask, in the order they would ask it.

Start with one sentence

[Startup name] helps [specific customer] [solve a specific problem] by [how you do it], unlike [what they use today].

Example: Campus Cabs helps students travel between campus and the city for less by matching them into shared cabs, unlike paying for a whole cab alone.

01 The deck

Twelve slides. One story.

Campus Cabs, an imaginary student startup, runs through every slide as an example.

Slide 0101 / 12

Title and one-liner

Who are you, and what does your startup do, in one sentence?

Put on it

  • Startup name and logo
  • Your one-liner: what you do and for whom
  • Presenter names and institute

Tip

Say the one-liner aloud while this slide is up. If the room remembers only this sentence, they should still understand what you do.

Avoid

A vague tagline like 'Revolutionising mobility' that could describe any company.

For Campus Cabs

Campus Cabs: shared rides between campus and the city, so students split the fare instead of paying for a whole cab.

Slide 0202 / 12

Problem

What painful problem exists, and who has it?

Put on it

  • Who exactly has the problem
  • When it happens and how often
  • What it costs them in money, time or effort

Tip

Describe one real moment from a user's day. A concrete scene is easier to remember than a general statement about an industry.

Avoid

Describing a problem so broadly that no specific person seems to have it.

For Campus Cabs

Students heading to the city often pay for a whole cab alone, even when classmates are making the same trip.

Slide 0303 / 12

Solution

How does your startup solve the problem you just described?

Put on it

  • Your solution in one or two plain sentences
  • How it removes each pain from the problem slide
  • The key insight that makes your approach work

Tip

Mirror the problem slide. Use the same words, so the audience can see exactly how your solution removes each pain point.

Avoid

Listing every feature instead of explaining the core idea that solves the problem.

For Campus Cabs

An app that matches students travelling the same route at the same time, so they share one cab and split the fare.

Slide 0404 / 12

Product or demo

What does the product actually look like, and does it work?

Put on it

  • Screenshots, a short video or a live demo
  • The main user journey from start to finish
  • What works today versus what is planned

Tip

Keep a recorded backup of your demo, saved offline. Wi-Fi and live apps can fail, and a recording lets you carry on.

Avoid

Presenting mock-ups as if they were a working product.

For Campus Cabs

Three screens: enter your destination and time, see who else is going, confirm the shared ride and fare split.

Slide 0505 / 12

Why now

Why is this the right moment for this startup?

Put on it

  • What has changed in technology, behaviour or cost
  • Why this was harder to do before
  • How your startup uses that change

Tip

Name a specific change you can explain and defend, such as a shift in user behaviour, a new technology or a cheaper tool.

Avoid

Saying 'the market is growing' without explaining what has actually changed.

For Campus Cabs

Students now pay each other instantly by phone, so splitting a fare is easy. An app can replace messy group-chat planning.

Slide 0606 / 12

Market

How many potential customers are there, and how big could this become?

Put on it

  • A bottom-up estimate you can explain line by line
  • The size of the first segment you will serve
  • The larger market you could grow into later
  • Sources for any figures you did not calculate yourself

Tip

Build your estimate from the bottom up and show the working. A small, believable number is more convincing than a large, unexplained one.

Avoid

Quoting a huge industry figure and claiming you only need a tiny share of it.

For Campus Cabs

Students on our campus who travel to the city, times trips per month, times our fee per trip. Repeat for nearby colleges.

Slide 0707 / 12

Business model

How will you make money?

Put on it

  • Who pays, and for what
  • Your price and how you arrived at it
  • Roughly what it costs you to deliver each sale
  • Later revenue ideas, clearly marked as future

Tip

If you have not charged anyone yet, say so and explain how you will test pricing. Honesty earns more trust than confident guesses.

Avoid

Saying 'we will monetise later', or listing many revenue streams with no clear focus.

For Campus Cabs

Each rider pays a small booking fee with their share of the fare. Later, possibly commission from partner cab operators.

Slide 0808 / 12

Traction and validation

What proof do you have that people want this?

Put on it

  • Customer interviews and the key things you learned
  • Pilot users, sign-ups, pre-orders or letters of intent
  • Progress over time, not just a single total
  • Honest results, including what did not work

Tip

Early evidence counts. Customer interviews, a waitlist or a small manual pilot show judges you have tested the idea with real people.

Avoid

Counting likes, followers or friends' opinions as proof that customers will pay.

For Campus Cabs

Before building the app, we matched rides by hand in a group chat and tracked how many riders booked again.

Slide 0909 / 12

Competition

Who else solves this problem, and why will customers choose you?

Put on it

  • Direct competitors and the workarounds people use today
  • A simple comparison on what users care about most
  • Your clear advantage
  • What would make that advantage hard to copy

Tip

Include how people solve the problem today, even if it is not a company. Doing nothing is a competitor too.

Avoid

Claiming you have no competitors. It suggests you have not looked closely at the market.

For Campus Cabs

Alternatives: solo cabs, public transport and group-chat arrangements. We are cheaper than a solo cab and easier than coordinating in chats.

Slide 1010 / 12

Go-to-market

How will you reach your first customers and then grow?

Put on it

  • The channel and message that will reach your first users
  • Who on the team is responsible for each channel
  • How you will keep users coming back and earn referrals
  • How you will repeat the approach with the next group

Tip

Start with users you can reach directly, through channels you already have, before paying for advertising.

Avoid

Saying 'social media marketing' with no specific channel, message or first target group.

For Campus Cabs

Launch through hostel groups and student clubs, offer referral credits, then repeat the playbook at nearby colleges.

Slide 1111 / 12

Team

Why is this team the right one to build this?

Put on it

  • Names, roles and a photo of each founder
  • Relevant skills, projects or experience
  • Why you personally care about this problem
  • Mentors or advisers, if you have any

Tip

Show why your team fits this problem, not just your branch and year. Mention what each person has already built or done.

Avoid

Listing titles like CEO and CTO with no evidence of what each person can do.

For Campus Cabs

Three students who make this trip often: one built the app, one ran the pilot, one manages driver partnerships.

Slide 1212 / 12

The ask

What do you want from the audience, and what will you do with it?

Put on it

  • What you need: funding, mentorship, incubation or introductions
  • How you will use it
  • The milestones it will help you reach
  • Your contact details, so people can follow up

Tip

Tie your ask to specific milestones, then close by repeating your one-liner so the audience leaves with your core message.

Avoid

Ending with 'Any questions?' without ever saying what you actually need from the audience.

For Campus Cabs

Mentorship on driver partnerships, plus funding to launch across our campus and run a pilot at a second college.

02 Design

Make it easy to read.

  1. One idea per slide

    Each slide should make one point. If a slide is trying to say two things, split it into two slides or cut one.

  2. Headlines that say something

    Write each slide's headline as a statement, such as 'Students overpay for solo cabs', not just a label like 'Problem'. Read in order, the headlines should tell your story.

  3. Few words, large text

    Use short phrases, not paragraphs, and text large enough to read from the back of the room. If you are reading a slide aloud, it probably has too much text.

  4. Show, do not tell

    Use screenshots, diagrams and simple charts where they explain faster than words. Every image should support the point of that slide, not decorate it.

  5. Keep it consistent

    Use one or two fonts, a small set of colours and the same layout throughout. A consistent deck looks organised and keeps attention on the content.

  6. Strong contrast

    Use dark text on a light background, or the reverse. Projectors can wash out colours, so test your deck on a projector before the event.

  7. Honest charts

    Label axes and units, start bar charts at zero, highlight the number that matters and state the source of any data you did not collect yourself.

  8. Slide numbers and appendix

    Number your slides and keep extra detail in an appendix after the ask. An appendix helps you answer judges' questions without cluttering the main deck.

03 Delivery

Say it out loud.

  1. Open with the point

    Start with the problem or your one-liner, not a long introduction. Introduce yourselves briefly and save backgrounds for the team slide.

  2. Rehearse against the clock

    Practise the full pitch aloud with a timer until it fits the limit with time to spare. Rehearse in front of people who will give honest feedback.

  3. Face the audience, not the screen

    Make eye contact with the people in the room. Glance at the slides only to stay on track; they support your pitch but do not deliver it.

  4. Slow down and pause

    Nerves often make people speak too fast. Slow down and pause after key moments, such as the problem and the ask, so they land.

  5. Plan your handovers

    If several teammates present, decide who covers which slides and which questions. Rehearse the handovers so the pitch flows as one story.

  6. Answer questions directly

    Listen to the whole question, answer it directly and keep it short. If you do not know, say so and explain how you would find out.

  7. Take feedback well

    Judges and mentors may challenge your idea. Thank them, note the feedback and do not get defensive. Their questions show what your deck needs to explain better.
